# 1. WHAT SCORECARDING ACTUALLY IS

Scorecarding is the practice of converting:

“Is this good?”

into:

“What does good mean, how important is each part, how well does this perform, what evidence supports that score, and what improvement would create the greatest increase in real-world value?”

A scorecard contains five things:

1. WHAT matters
2. HOW MUCH it matters
3. WHAT excellent means
4. HOW well the work performs
5. WHAT should improve next

The score is the measurement.

The improvement is the purpose.

# 2. THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE

## WEIGHT THE THINGS THAT MATTER MOST.

A category worth 20% should have substantially more influence than a category worth 2%.

That means:

A beautiful product that doesn't work should not score highly.

A technically perfect product nobody understands should not score highly.

A persuasive marketing campaign that produces no meaningful action should not score highly.

A highly accurate research system that takes ten hours to use may have a serious usability problem.

A gorgeous AI image that communicates the wrong message is not excellent.

**Excellence is multidimensional.**

# 3. THE UNIVERSAL 10-POINT SCALE

Every category receives a score from 1–10.

### 1 — Broken

Fails fundamentally.

### 2 — Very Poor

Major problems. Barely useful.

### 3 — Weak

Works in limited circumstances but has serious deficiencies.

### 4 — Below Standard

Some value exists, but important problems remain.

### 5 — Functional

Acceptable. Works, but nothing exceptional.

### 6 — Good

Clearly useful and reasonably competent.

### 7 — Strong

Above average. Few meaningful weaknesses.

### 8 — Excellent

Highly effective. Minor improvements remain.

### 9 — Exceptional

Professional-grade. Very difficult to improve materially.

### 10 — Best-in-Class

Exceptional across the category, validated by evidence, and difficult to improve without changing the nature of the solution.

### IMPORTANT

A 10 does not mean:

> “I can't think of anything else.”

It means:

> **“Given the purpose, audience, constraints, evidence, and available resources, this currently represents an exceptional standard of execution.”**

# 4. THE WEIGHTED SCORE

For each category:

`Category Score × Category Weight`

Then add every weighted category together.

Example:

| Category | Weight | Score | Weighted |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Functionality | 25% | 9 | 2.25 |
| UX | 20% | 8 | 1.60 |
| Visual | 15% | 9 | 1.35 |
| Accuracy | 20% | 10 | 2.00 |
| Performance | 10% | 8 | 0.80 |
| Accessibility | 10% | 9 | 0.90 |
| TOTAL | 100% | | **8.90** |

Final score:

**8.9 / 10**

The weights prevent low-priority strengths from hiding critical weaknesses.

# 8. THE CROSS-PROJECT QUALITY GATES

Here's another important distinction.

Some things shouldn't compete with the domain categories.

They should be **gates**.

A beautiful product should never get a 9.8 if it creates an unacceptable safety problem.

Therefore every serious scorecard should consider:

### SAFETY

Could this cause foreseeable harm?

### TRUST

Are claims honest and appropriately supported?

### PRIVACY

Is personal or sensitive information handled appropriately?

### SECURITY

Could foreseeable misuse compromise the system or user?

### LEGAL / ETHICAL

Are there material legal or ethical concerns?

### ACCESSIBILITY

Can people with different abilities actually use it?

These aren't necessarily weighted into every domain score.

They can be **release gates**.

For example:

> If critical safety = fail → DO NOT SHIP.

That is much more logical than letting 95 points of beautiful design compensate for one catastrophic safety failure.

---

# 9. THE BOTTLENECK RULE

A weighted average can hide a serious weakness.

Therefore:

## NEVER LOOK ONLY AT THE FINAL SCORE.

After calculating the weighted score, identify:

### 1. LOWEST CATEGORY

What is weakest?

### 2. HIGHEST-WEIGHT LOW SCORE

What weakness has the greatest effect on the outcome?

### 3. HIGHEST-LEVERAGE IMPROVEMENT

What single improvement would create the greatest increase in real-world value?

That becomes the next task.

---

# 10. THE “WHY IS THIS NOT A 10?” TEST

After every evaluation:

Ask:

> Why is this a 9 instead of a 10?

Then:

> Why is it a 9 instead of a 9.5?

Then:

> What specific change would move the score?

Avoid vague answers such as:

“Make it better.”

Instead:

“Reduce the number of steps from seven to three.”

“Improve evidence quality.”

“Fix the mobile navigation.”

“Add error recovery.”

“Clarify the CTA.”

“Remove the redundant section.”

“Improve source verification.”

That turns criticism into engineering.

# 11. SCORECARD EVIDENCE LEVELS

A score should be based on the strongest evidence available.

### LEVEL 0 — Opinion

“I think it's good.”

Weakest.

### LEVEL 1 — Inspection

“I inspected it and found…”

Better.

### LEVEL 2 — Comparison

“It performs better against the defined standard…”

Better.

### LEVEL 3 — Testing

“I tested the important states…”

Strong.

### LEVEL 4 — Real User Evidence

“Real users successfully completed the intended task…”

Very strong.

### LEVEL 5 — Measured Real-World Outcome

“The system demonstrably improved the intended outcome…”

Strongest.

The higher the score, the stronger the evidence should generally be.

# 15. THE 10/10 RELEASE STANDARD

A product should not automatically ship simply because its average is 9.5.

Use three conditions:

### A. OVERALL SCORE

Target:

**≥ 9.5**

### B. CRITICAL CATEGORIES

No critical category below the defined minimum.

Recommended:

**≥ 9.0**

### C. RELEASE GATES

No unresolved critical:

- safety
- security
- privacy
- accessibility
- legal
- data integrity

failures.

Therefore:

## 9.7 WITH A CRITICAL FAILURE ≠ SHIP

But:

## 9.5 WITH NO CRITICAL FAILURES + STRONG EVIDENCE = SHIP CANDIDATE

# 20. THE DEEPEST PRINCIPLE

Scorecarding isn't about becoming obsessed with numbers.

The number is simply a way to force clarity.

The real goal is:

**SEE BETTER → THINK BETTER → BUILD BETTER → SERVE BETTER**

A scorecard should make hidden problems visible.

It should prevent beautiful mediocrity.

It should prevent technically impressive uselessness.

It should prevent confident nonsense.

It should prevent “good enough” from quietly becoming the final product.

And most importantly:

> **It should help us discover what we couldn't see before we measured it.**

# 22. CANONICAL IMPLEMENTATION RULE

This document is not merely educational material. It is an operating standard.

When Naya, MAXESS, or another Human Maximus system evaluates work:

1. Start with the universal method.
2. Identify the actual outcome and failure conditions.
3. Select the specialized scorecard when a domain matches.
4. Adapt the categories when the job materially differs.
5. Assign weights based on real importance, not symmetry.
6. Make the weights total exactly 100%.
7. Score from evidence, not enthusiasm.
8. Apply cross-project release gates where appropriate.
9. Identify the highest-leverage bottleneck.
10. Improve the work rather than manipulating the score.
11. Re-score.
12. Prove the result.
13. Freeze only the version that has earned its score.

### The scorecard is a tool.

### The standard is the system.

### The improvement is the purpose.

### The proof is the permission to ship.
